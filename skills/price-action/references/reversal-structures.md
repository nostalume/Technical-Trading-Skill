# Reversal and boundary structures

Use this reference when evaluating a named structure rather than single-bar
geometry. Treat the names as conditional descriptions: identify their components,
alternatives, missing observations, and time of availability. The bundle does not
automatically recognize any of these complex patterns.

## Do not confuse a pivot with a reversal

A strict local extremum depends on an explicit neighborhood. Its confirmation
requires the right neighbors, their closure, and the applicable evidence cutoff;
the center bar's timestamp is not its confirmation time. A confirmed local high
does not itself establish resistance, a meaningful swing, or a trend reversal.
An outside center can satisfy both local high and low relations without revealing
an intrabar sequence. Do not order those extrema by inference.

For a reversal interpretation, name the original directional premise and what
has contradicted it. A counter-move can be a pullback, expansion of a range, or an
attempt at a new directional sequence. Price crossing a line and price sustaining
a new sequence are different observations. Don't retroactively attribute later
confirmation to an earlier decision.

## Wedge and three pushes

The source uses **wedge** for a three-push hypothesis, with identifiable reactions
between pushes and often converging candidate boundaries. List the three pushes
and their endpoints. Compare incremental progress and retreat, not just three
arbitrary highs/lows; do not force convergence by redrawing anchors after the
fact. Three pushes name the proposed structure, not a profitable entry rule.

Separate declining incremental progress from accelerating/parabolic progression.
They are different observations even when the source applies related wedge
language. A shrinking advance can reflect a pause or a boundary limit, not proof
of exhausted buyers. A developing third push remains a candidate until its
relevant components are observable.

Direction of the formation is not its breakout direction. A downward three-push
retreat inside an upward context can support a pullback-completion hypothesis;
the same local shape inside an established decline can instead be an end-of-leg
or continuation question. Inspect boundary break, later response, extreme test,
and renewed continuation rather than applying a fixed bar-count distinction
between "wedge pullback" and "wedge reversal."

For **micro** or **nested** wedges, identify the actual smaller interval or
embedded component. These terms do not provide extra independent confirmation.
Without identifiable legs, return a descriptive observation rather than a forced
wedge label. The existence of a wedge does not ban all continuation or reversal
ideas; suitability remains a separate, user-scoped question.

## Final flag vs ordinary pause

A **final-flag candidate (FF)** is a consolidation hypothesized to be late in an
existing directional sequence. Name the prior sequence, horizontal/overlapping
consolidation interval, and any supplied nearby extreme/projection. Its position
near a projection is context, not evidence that the projection will be reached.

"Final" is partly an outcome description. Before subsequent evidence arrives,
compare ordinary pause/continuation, renewed range formation, and failed
continuation. A move out of the candidate flag and then back in can contradict a
continuation premise; it does not complete a larger reversal by itself. Don't
require a fixed flag duration or equate any flag after a spike with a failed FF.
Mixed overlap and late-trend readings can coexist without a forced state route.

## Double top/bottom and neckline

For a double-top/bottom candidate, identify first extreme, intervening retreat,
second test, price comparison, and subsequent response. A proposed **neckline**
comes from the intervening structure, not an arbitrary round price. A single wick
is not two tests; two adjacent prices without a reaction are not automatically a
full double formation.

If "near equal" matters, state the actual difference and the chosen scale or
explicit tolerance. No universal tick count or ATR epsilon defines closeness.
A slight excursion and return, a close beyond, and sustained continuation beyond
the old extreme can imply different hypotheses. Define the failure premise
rather than assuming that every second contact is a failed breakout.

The intervening level breaking, a later retest, and a new swing sequence are
separate observations. A micro-double candidate can provide local test context,
not automatic effective support/resistance or proof of a major reversal.

## Major trend reversal (MTR) as a component claim

In the source's framework, a stronger major-reversal hypothesis relates:

- an identifiable original trend;
- a break of an explicitly anchored trend/channel boundary;
- evidence that attempted original-trend resumption did not restore the premise;
- a test of the earlier extreme with an identified failure response.

Trace these relations and their times. One bar can contribute to more than one
component, but do not count that as independent evidence. If a line or an extreme
test is absent, say which component is absent and use "reversal attempt" or a
plain description instead of claiming a complete source-defined MTR.

A completed set of source components is still a hypothesis, not a guarantee that
the new trend persists. Compare a larger pullback/range interpretation and inspect
whether subsequent advances/retreats support a new directional sequence. A second
attempt can add context but is not a compulsory fifth component or probability
upgrade. Do not retain the old universal MTR trading ban or its quoted success
rates as facts about the current case.

## Triangles and changing boundaries

Identify ordered boundary test points and the line/level interpretation:

| Description | Proposed boundary relation | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Ascending triangle | Approximately level upper tests with rising lower tests. | A guaranteed upward break or permission to buy before one occurs. |
| Descending triangle | Approximately level lower tests with falling upper tests. | A guaranteed downward break or permission to short the product. |
| Symmetrical triangle | Lower upper tests and higher lower tests, converging. | A chosen breakout direction. |
| Expanding formation | Upper/lower tests diverge and the observed envelope expands. | A universal ban, a stable range, or known intrabar order. |

With too few or ambiguous anchors, describe convergence/divergence rather than
fit a categorical name. Report an approximation method if the conclusion depends
on it. Wedge and triangle conventions can overlap; the source's three-push and
boundary relations explain the chosen wording, not an exclusive classifier.
Assess any eventual boundary crossing using an event-time baseline and later
response. The geometric widest-range projection is not proof of reachability.

## Projection and supplied-plan review boundary

A measured move (**MM**) is an equal-distance projection from explicit endpoints
or a range, with an explicit anchor and direction. The script only performs that
geometry. It does not select a completed leg, fit channel/triangle lines, decide
which candidate deserves priority, or validate an entry/stop/target plan.

When explaining a supplied projection, identify its selected height, endpoint
order, anchor, and alternative nearby structures. Do not use an unordered recent
high/low pair to claim both a completed upward and downward leg. If comparing a
supplied plan, preserve its prices and disclose that gross RR does not establish
structural invalidation, transaction costs, probability, or actual fillability.
When the user requests a candidate or supplied-plan update, the entry routes to
the dedicated planning reference; a structure label alone does not select prices.
